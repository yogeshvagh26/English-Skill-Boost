## 1. Situation : Investor Pitch – The "Show Me the Money" Demand

**Context:** You are a Senior Data Analyst at a fast-growing SaaS startup. You're in a boardroom with your CEO (Sarah) and three potential investors (VCs). They've seen your pitch deck, they like the product, but they're skeptical about the financial projections. One investor, a seasoned VC named Marcus, leans forward and says: "Your revenue projections look aggressive. Show me the data that backs them up. I want to see the math." You need to present the financial story with confidence, clarity, and credibility.

## 3. Vocabulary and Expressions

| Word/Phrase                     | Part of Speech | Meaning                                                                                | Example Sentence                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------- | -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Financial model**             | Noun Phrase    | A mathematical representation of a company's financial performance and projections     | *Our financial model is based on three pillars: historical, market, and unit economics.*          |
| **Unit economics**              | Noun Phrase    | The profitability of a single unit (e.g., a customer) over their lifetime              | *Unit economics show that we generate $5 for every $1 we invest.*                                 |
| **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)** | Acronym/Metric | The cost of acquiring a new customer (sales + marketing)                               | *Our CAC is $1,200—we need to keep it below $1,500 to maintain healthy margins.*                  |
| **Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)** | Acronym/Metric | The total revenue expected from a customer over their entire relationship with the company | *Our LTV is $6,000—a 5x ratio to CAC, which is healthy.*                                         |
| **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)** | Acronym/Metric | The percentage of recurring revenue retained from existing customers, including expansions | *Our NRR is 110%—meaning existing customers spend more over time.*                                |
| **CAC payback period**          | Noun Phrase    | The time it takes to recover the cost of acquiring a customer                          | *Our payback period is 8 months—that's much better than the industry average of 12–18 months.*    |
| **Scenario modeling**           | Noun Phrase    | Creating multiple versions of a financial projection (base, downside, upside)          | *Scenario modeling shows we can withstand a worst-case 2x growth and still be profitable.*       |
| **Cash runway**                 | Noun Phrase    | The amount of time a company can operate at its current burn rate with its current cash | *Our cash runway is 18 months—which gives us time to grow into our model.*                        |
| **Market expansion**            | Noun Phrase    | Entering new markets (new regions, new customer segments, etc.)                        | *Market expansion into Europe is validated by 15 pilot customers with 90% retention.*            |
| **Historical performance**      | Noun Phrase    | Past financial and operational results, used to predict future performance             | *Our historical performance shows 2.2x growth—a proven pattern.*                                  |
| **Inbound interest**            | Noun Phrase    | Demand from potential customers who reach out to you (vs. outbound sales)             | *Inbound interest from Europe increased 40% last quarter—a leading indicator of demand.*        |
| **Validation**                  | Noun           | Proving that a product or market opportunity is real through real customer data        | *Pilot customers with 90% retention validate our product-market fit in new markets.*             |

## 4. Key Takeaways

### Common Sentence Patterns (for Investor Pitch Storytelling)

| Pattern                                                              | Use                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| *"I've built our model on three pillars: (1) [X], (2) [Y], (3) [Z]."* | To structure the financial story clearly.                                              |
| *"First, [X]. Over the last [timeframe], we've [achievement]."*       | To present historical evidence with concrete numbers.                                  |
| *"Second, [Y]. Our data shows [positive signal]—we've validated it through [evidence]."* | To support market expansion claims with data.                                          |
| *"Third, [Z]. Our [metric] is [X], which is [benchmark comparison]."*  | To present unit economics with industry context.                                       |
| *"I've modeled three scenarios: base case, downside case, upside case."* | To acknowledge risk and show preparedness.                                             |
| *"Even in the downside case, we're [positive outcome]."*              | To reassure investors that the business is resilient.                                  |
| *"The story isn't [simplistic claim]. The story is [nuanced, data-backed narrative]."* | To reframe the narrative (anchor pattern).                                             |

### Useful Phrases to Remember
- **When an investor questions your projections:** *"That's a fair question—let me show you the math."*
- **Introducing unit economics:** *"Let me show you the unit economics—this is how we know we can scale efficiently."*
- **When you're not sure of a number:** *"That number is based on [specific assumption]. I can adjust it easily and show you the impact."*
- **Building credibility:** *"I've included sensitivity tables showing how changes in key assumptions affect the outcome."*
- **When the investor asks a tough question:** *"I'm glad you asked that—it's one of the risks we've modeled."*
- **Closing the pitch:** *"With your investment, we can execute this plan and achieve the growth we've modeled. I'm confident in our ability to deliver."*

### Practical Speaking Tips (for Investor Pitch Narratives)
1.  **Start with a "North Star" metric.** Show one number that captures the investment opportunity (e.g., $33M ARR in 3 years, 5x LTV/CAC ratio). This anchors the conversation.
2.  **Show the "Three Pillars" of your model.** Investors want to know: (1) Have you done this before? (historical), (2) Is there more market? (market expansion), (3) Is it profitable to scale? (unit economics). Address all three.
3.  **Use a "waterfall" chart for revenue growth.** Show starting revenue, then add new customers, expansion, and churn—to get to final revenue. This makes the model transparent and believable.
4.  **Acknowledge your "downside scenario."** Investors trust you more if you tell them what could go wrong. Say: *"If we hit headwinds, we still grow 2x and are profitable."*
5.  **Quantify the "Cash Runway."** Investors always ask about burn rate and cash runway. Have this number ready and explain how you'll use the investment to extend it.
6.  **Use "Sensitivity Analysis."** Show how revenue changes if customer acquisition cost or churn is 10% higher or lower. This shows you've considered uncertainty.
7.  **Practice your "30-second pitch."** If you only have 30 seconds to convince an investor, what would you say? Practice it until it's perfect.
8.  **Be ready for "pivot" questions.** Investors may test your assumptions. Say: *"We've thought about that—here's what the data shows."*
9.  **End with a "Decision Request."** Say: *"I'm asking for a $5M investment. With it, we'll achieve $33M ARR in 3 years. If we don't hit our targets, we have a 18-month cash runway to adjust."*
10. **Follow up with a "Data Room."** After the pitch, provide a data room with all the supporting spreadsheets, assumptions, and external benchmarks. This builds trust and diligence.
