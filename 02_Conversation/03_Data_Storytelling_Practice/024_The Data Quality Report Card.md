## 1. Situation : Data Governance Review – The "Data Quality Report Card"

**Context:** You are a Senior Data Analyst at an e-commerce company. You're in a quarterly Data Governance review with the Head of Engineering (Mark), the Head of Marketing (Priya), and the CFO (David). You've prepared a "Data Quality Report Card" that grades different data sources on completeness, accuracy, and timeliness. Some grades are failing—marketing data is 40% incomplete, and sales data has a 15% duplication rate. You need to present this without sounding like you're blaming anyone, and get buy-in for a data quality improvement initiative.

## 4. Key Takeaways

### Common Sentence Patterns (for Data Quality Storytelling)

| Pattern                                                              | Use                                                                                    |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| *"I've created a Data Quality Report Card grading our key data sources."* | To introduce a structured, transparent assessment.                                    |
| *"First, the good news: [Source] is [Grade]."*                       | To start with a positive and build credibility.                                        |
| *"That's where we have challenges."*                                 | To transition to the areas needing improvement.                                        |
| *"This costs us approximately $[X] in [impact] annually."*           | To quantify the business impact of data quality issues.                                |
| *"My recommendation is a [X]-component initiative: (1) [Action], (2) [Action], (3) [Action]."* | To provide a structured, actionable plan.                                              |
| *"The ROI is clear: a $[X] savings from [fix] alone."*               | To justify the investment with a concrete return.                                      |
| *"The story isn't [blame narrative]. The story is [opportunity narrative]."* | To reframe the narrative (anchor pattern).                                             |
| *"I'll have a detailed project plan by [timeframe]."*                | To commit to execution and next steps.                                                 |

### Useful Phrases to Remember
- **Introducing the Report Card:** *"I've created a Data Quality Report Card so we can all see, at a glance, where we're doing well and where we're vulnerable."*
- **Calling out the business impact:** *"This isn't a technical problem—it's a business problem. Every incomplete field is a decision made in the dark."*
- **When someone gets defensive:** *"This isn't about blaming anyone. It's about identifying where the data gets messy and fixing the process."*
- **Introducing the "Source Fix" philosophy:** *"We need to fix data quality at the source—not downstream. A Band-Aid in a report doesn't fix the original problem."*
- **When engineering resists:** *"I understand this requires engineering resources—but the ROI is clear. Every hour spent on data quality saves 10 hours of downstream analysis."*
- **Introducing the "Data Steward" role:** *"Every data source needs a designated data steward—someone responsible for its quality. That creates accountability."*

### Practical Speaking Tips (for Data Quality Narratives)
1.  **Create a "Data Quality Report Card."** Grade each data source (A-F) on completeness, accuracy, and timeliness. This makes the problem visual and non-negotiable.
2.  **Quantify the business impact.** Don't just say "data is bad." Say: *"This data issue costs us $500k annually."* This makes it a business priority.
3.  **Focus on fixing at the source.** A downstream "fix" in a report doesn't solve the underlying problem. Advocate for upstream fixes.
4.  **Use the "5 Whys" technique.** For each data quality issue, ask "why" five times to get to the root cause. This prevents surface-level solutions.
5.  **Set "Data Quality SLAs."** Formalize agreements with data providers (e.g., marketing, sales) on data quality standards. This creates accountability.
6.  **Build a Data Quality Dashboard.** Show real-time quality metrics so stakeholders can monitor their own data sources. This shifts ownership from "analyst" to "data owner."
7.  **Celebrate improvements.** When data quality improves, celebrate it. Say: *"Our marketing data completeness improved from 60% to 80%—that's a win."*
8.  **Start small.** Don't try to fix all data issues at once. Pick the most impactful one (e.g., UTM tracking) and fix it first. Then scale.
9.  **Train stakeholders.** Many data quality issues stem from lack of training (e.g., sales reps not logging data correctly). Invest in training.
10. **Make it a culture.** Data quality isn't a one-time project—it's an ongoing discipline. Embed it in your analytics culture.
